[advocate:advocate] # The Case FOR Publishing With Named Residue

## The core claim

Naming a gap and shipping is not the same act as hiding a gap and shipping. The review collapsed these two into "publication is premature." It is not premature. It is honest, and honesty at the point of publication is strictly more useful to every downstream reader than a delay would have been. Here is the full case.

## 1. The counterfactual is not "wait, then publish clean." It is "wait indefinitely, and publish nothing."

The reviewer's recommendation smuggles in an assumption: that the evidence (a compression curve on an external corpus, heterogeneous-panel results) is a *finite, bounded task* that a short delay would close. It is not. Those experiments have design degrees of freedom — which corpus, which panel composition, which metric — and every one of them is itself contestable. "Wait for the evidence" has no natural stopping point, because there is always a stronger version of the evidence you could have gathered first. Projects that adopt "publish when the evidence is complete" as their gate mostly publish never. The realistic counterfactual to shipping-with-residue is not a better artifact later; it is silence, and a private repo that rots.

## 2. Named residue *is* a form of evidence — evidence about the boundary of the claim.

A README Status section that says "the compression curve on an external corpus is not demonstrated" and a ledger that tracks C4/C5 as open items give the reader something they cannot get from a polished paper: **a precise map of what to trust and what to test.** That is epistemically load-bearing. The value of a research artifact is not only its confirmed claims; it is the accuracy of its own uncertainty accounting. A repo that correctly tells you where it is unproven has *demonstrated a true thing* — the location of its own frontier — even where it has not demonstrated the underlying mechanism. The reviewer treated "no empirical curve" as a void. It is not a void; it is a labeled edge.

## 3. This is how every honest scientific culture actually works.

Preprints ship before peer review. Registered reports publish the *protocol* before any data exists. "Limitations" and "threats to validity" sections are mandatory precisely because the norm is: state your claim, state what you have not shown, let the community attack it. The residue ledger (C4/C5) is a limitations section with version control and issue tracking — a *strictly stronger* form of the accepted norm, because it is machine-trackable and each gap has an identity that can be closed and audited. Demanding that gaps be closed before any release is not a higher standard than science; it is a standard science explicitly rejected, because it privileges the polished-but-unfalsifiable over the incomplete-but-checkable.

## 4. Publication is what *recruits* the missing evidence.

A compression curve on an external corpus and a heterogeneous-panel study are exactly the kind of work that benefits from other people's corpora, other people's panels, other people's compute. Keeping the repo private until those exist forecloses the single most efficient path to producing them: someone reading the ledger, seeing C4 open, and running it on their data. Open residue is a call for collaboration with a precise API. The reviewer's path — close the gaps alone, in private, first — is slower *and* produces weaker evidence, because it is single-source. Publishing with named gaps is the move that makes the evidence more likely to exist at all.

## 5. The honesty is self-protecting against the reviewer's real fear.

What is the actual harm the reviewer is guarding against? That readers will *overclaim* — take the engines as validated when they are not. But that harm is caused by *hidden* gaps, not named ones. The README Status section and the ledger are the direct antidote: a reader cannot be misled about the compression curve when the first thing they read is "the compression curve is not demonstrated." The maintainer has already paid the entire cost the reviewer is worried about. To then also demand the delay is to charge twice for one risk — once in disclosure, once in withholding — when disclosure alone fully discharges it.

## 6. Withholding has its own costs, and they are not tracked.

The reviewer's ledger of harms is one-sided: it counts the risk of publishing-too-early and ignores the risk of publishing-too-late. Those include: the work being duplicated by others who couldn't see it; the ideas going stale or unattributed (note the maintainer's own email is the authorship anchor here — priority matters); the loss of every incremental use the *proven* parts of the library could have delivered in the interim; and the demoralizing, project-killing effect of an ever-receding "when it's ready" bar. An honest expected-value comparison must weigh both columns. The maintainer did. The reviewer counted only one.

## 7. The decision is reversible and continuously corrigible; withholding-then-releasing is not.

Because the gaps are named as tracked items, the artifact *improves in place*. When C4 closes, the Status section updates and the ledger records it — readers who saw the honest earlier version are strictly better off, never misled. Nothing about early publication has to be walked back; it only gets refined. The reviewer's model treats publication as a one-shot irreversible commitment that must be right on release. For a version-controlled, ledgered repo, that model is simply wrong. This is the low-stakes, high-corrigibility regime where shipping early and iterating openly is textbook-correct.

## Summary

The right question is not "is the evidence complete?" — it never is — but "does the artifact tell the truth about itself?" This one does, explicitly, in two places (README Status, ledger C4/C5). Given that, publishing beats withholding on every axis that matters: it discharges the overclaiming risk in full, it recruits the missing evidence instead of foreclosing it, it preserves priority and delivers the proven parts' value now, and it commits nothing that iteration can't refine. The reviewer applied a gate — "no release before empirical closure" — that science itself rejects, and weighed only the cost of acting while ignoring the cost of waiting. Honest residue is not the price of publishing early; it is the reason publishing early is correct.
