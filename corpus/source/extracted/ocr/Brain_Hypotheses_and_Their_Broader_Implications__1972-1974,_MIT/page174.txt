The Collected Works of Patrick Gunkel

Book, “Brain Hypotheses and Their Broader Implications" (1972-4)
Part V, “Evolution”

Chapter:

"Why Men May Outsmart Whales”

Along a number of indexes (e.g. degree of size and convolution of cortex,
ratio of neocortex to whole cortex, size of cerebellar cortex, etc) one might
assume that the whale ought to outrank man intellectually or at least ought
to be in the same boat.

So far we know rather little about the structure, much less physiology and
chemistry, of the giant whale and elephant brains, absolutely or in measured
comparison to man. This means, in a way, that we really don't know what
we're talking about when we assume that whales ought to be so smart.

But we do know many things and many of the important things. Admittedly
no one seems to have added these up and correlated them on paper, which in a
way is the purpose of this brief article.

When that is done, one can immediately see - e.g. via the grouping between
indexes - why cetaceans might well be mentally inferior to ourselves, at
least in the power to solve problems or tendency to influence the world.

Synoptically, the cetaceans may fail, as intellectual competitors to
ourselves, because they are inferior in at least the following respects:

1) Cortical lamination is the poorest of studied mammals, poorer than the
rabbit. Oppositely, it is probably generally highest in man in
fibroarchitectonic and cytoarchitectonic terms, in the state of development
of both newer and older areas (Callocortic and mesocortic - excepting certain
paleocortic and semicortic areas; which in certain cases, however, may
simultaneously increase in size toward man).

2) Regional variations of cell type,

3) and in lamination, are poor or even lacking in the cortex.

4) Typological differentiation of cortical neurons is inferior.

5) Cerebral cortex is thinner.

6) Although neuron density in dolphin cortex is less than 1/3 the rat,
rabbit, and monkey, and such density decreases evolutionarily and in man, its
low dolphin index may nevertheless have a tendency to retire optimistic
notions based on the larger cortex (Cespecially when the poor cellular
differentiation is considered).

7) Although the dendritic spines of cortical neurons are perhaps enlarged
in cetaceans, by comparison with other animals, they are not especially
numerous.

8) It is often argued that the hippocampus is primitive and evolutionarily
regressive, but this is relative to the neocortex: its absolute cortical
surface area increases through the primates and is largest in man of ‘any'
animal (1863 mmA2, despite the regression of the primary olfactory system).
(Oddly, however, it is larger in the dog than the macaque: 416 vs. 282 mmA2.)
Yet IF ONE IMAGINED a dolphin with a cortex the same size as man's, its area
might be only 37% of what one would expect it to be in the latter. (The
state in man is confused by virtue of [size and qualitative] changes in [both
transverse and longitudinal] hippocampal subfields. I am, in candor,
uncomfortable with the use I have made of this item.)

9) Thalamic anterior nucleus.

10) Hypothalamic mammillary body is small.

11) In man, cortical Brodmann areas [32 and 31] >> [24 and 23], whereas in
dolphin <<.

12) Poor definition Cand size?) of the [medial, tuberal, lateral, and
posterior] hypothalamic nuclei.

Unfortunately most of these remarks are based on the brains of dolphins
rather than those of the largest whales. (Dolphins are technically whales.)
Yet the brain of at least one species of whale is six times larger than our
own!

One thing especially disappointing about the dolphin brain, which I did
not mention, is that a smaller percentage of its brain, than of the human
brain, represents prefrontal cortex. Yet the latter is considered to be the
highest organ of intelligence, at least in the primate order; and there are
good reasons why it ought to have this status in the cetacean order as well.
