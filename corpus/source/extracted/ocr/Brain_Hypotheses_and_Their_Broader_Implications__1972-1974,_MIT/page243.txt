Multifunctionalism. ordinarily it is thought or it is assumed in theories
that structures of the brain must have only one or one main function or
mode of operation, that they do or can have but one; or, if they have more,
that the number mist be very small and unusual, and perhaps exacts a cost F
or that the function mst be very clearly evidenced in the structure or,
particularly, in the differences and Similarities with other structures;
or, if there are many functions » that these must alternate and that they
must be sequentially and purposefully linked; furthermore, there is a habit
of looking for maximally contrasting and certainly dissimilar explanations
of function for different structures 3; there is also a certain tendency to
limit function on the basis of structure or to limit it arbitrarily. The
truth is that there may be, that it may even be the rule, that structures
may have an indefinite number of indepatient, semiindependent, and dependent
functions, and functions paying negligible attention to, at least obvious,
structural peculiarities and differences «.

Multistructuralism. Obversely, functions may be carried out by the integral
or duplicative work of many different structures, wheneas there is often a
tendency of workers to confine in their projections functions to unique and
sole structures, The miltiplicity of possible structural bases in a tissue,
by theory or fact, able to explain assumed functions is just overwhelming,
almost regardless of the functions to which structures would be ascribed;
and this holds for structures both large and tiny in the anatomic hierarchy.
In connexion with both mltistructuralism and multifunctionalism, it is
important to keep in mind that the addition of new structures and funetions
may have a radical bearing on the old, and that this too warrants a valid
theory.
