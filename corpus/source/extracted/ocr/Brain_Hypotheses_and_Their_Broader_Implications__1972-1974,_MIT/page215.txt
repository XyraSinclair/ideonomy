A NOTE GN THE RELATIVE SIMPLICITY AND COMPIEXITY GF ADEQUATE ENGRAMS
Pat Gunkel

Statements of mine have been potentially misleading where I have said
that the brain may be interested in the analysis and recordation of
virtual infinities of complexity and that this ‘interest' may be its
secret. Using both empirically selected components and mathematical
transformations, eg Fourier, surprisingly powerful discriminators of
universes of forms and the natural universe may be retrieved and applied
as the brain's ordinary operations

Consider particular values that might be assicned to stand for sensa.
Assume the sensum is a square. The values at branches of a decision tree
might be ideal middles for extreme ranges of wholly dissimilar classifications.
4s I have previously discussed, random continua might be at work so that
the classifications would be randomly and evenly interdistributed. Moreover,
ideal middles might be selected for particular situations. This extreme
and free distribution would have transcendent merit in classifying random
subsequent forms or the variations of sensa. Now the idea of "random
schemata" suggests that there are peculiar groups common to pseudorandom
systems of classification, within the infinity of possible parallel
classifications, so that these groups have a certain penetrancy (and an
horizon with properties that I cannot yet describe) and powerful linkage
between seemingly foreign forms is exploitable: moreover, these orderings
apparently partake of a certain universality. It is a pity that I cannot
introduce these, intuitively correct, concepts in a plain form. In any
event, there are many, seperable points in this article. It is conceivable
that, in terms of the foregone idea of two types of middles, the brain
develops in the direction of simplest discriminatory engrams, or something
like a "flat tree". It is of course possible that there may be many such
engrams ‘per sensum', or what might be called a "flat banvontzes « Here,
too, I have yet to wnravel the consequences and choose the order of
probability. With regard to the above, an extremely blurred form might
nevertheless serve recognition equally well as a sharp presentation of that
forms there is evidence to attest for this.

It is worth stating the contrasting idea of a "narrow, tall tree™: a
long series of emergent transformations. It is interesting to consider the
idea of an infinite tree of this kind: for practical purposes, the brain
might use merely practical extremes with no pretense of finish or perfection:
infinite narrowness suggests very few aspects: successive aspects might be
discrete or continuous, also iterative or different: infinite tallness suggests
innumerable transformations: either way, of interest are what convergences
would oecur en route over the tree. It is a pity we do not know what patterns
are in the brain.

We do not know how many dimensions the brain uses and what therefore is its
representative simplicity. One day we may discover that the brain of man and
the brain of the insect differ little in fundamental representation and
fundamental thought.

These matters, eg stress on processing versus stress on representative
complexity, are critically important for understanding the elementals for
machine thought. All of us may be surprised.
