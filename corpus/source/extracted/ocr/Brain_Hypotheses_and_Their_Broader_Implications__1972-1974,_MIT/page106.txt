"Arve An Inebriated Man and An Animal
Comparable Intellectually?"

Patrick Gunkel

I wish to suggest in this paper that a drunk and animal are analogous
in intelligence, inasmuch as a ‘congenital’ drunk would never achieve our
intelligence if he were as drunk as an animal is dumb (assuming that this
is pointful).

It is perhaps significant that damage to the dorsomedial nucleus of the
thalamus, for example, produces a kind of drunkenness, and that there is
some indication, however slight, that damage of this kind, if congenital,
would produce idiocy.

Characteristics of these states are: obvious stupidity, amnesia,
silliness, confusion, disorientation, de-concentration (i.e., loss of
concentration), defect of purpose, vulgarity, defect of insight, irreality,
defect of the sense of time, impairment of consciousness, visceral changes,
etc.

Here is what I must report from being--experimentally--drunk: motor
incoordination, slowness of thought, muddleheadedness, retarded
perception, lapsing and restriction of perception, a kind of closing and
shrinking of the world, autocosm (i.e., a self-created microcosm or private
world, or an equivalent subjective state), poorness of memory, difficulty in
thinking systematically, decrease of purpose, childishness, emotional
blunting and crudification, a lack of seriousness or care, overgeneralized
pleasure (i.e., inability to package and direct pleasure, and to postpone
pleasure and things), simplification of perception, greater illusion,
diminished self-control, aprosexia, excessive perceived harmony of things,
a centering on and yet ‘thinning’ of the present (e.g., a ‘loss of the past and
future’), dissociation of behavior, mental inexactitude or blurredness, loss
of curiosity or habituation (shallow curiosity), overconcern with ‘simply
getting on in the world’ (i.e., with orientation, action, reaction, and
coping), conception yielding to perception or mere sensation, overcertainty,
poor perception of self (i.e. de-objectification), glibness, loss of spontaneity
(e.g., in extremis, "stiffness’), etc.

Now these are very interesting properties, if one assumes that they
could be dimensionally (i.e., as dimensions) inverted by changes in
nervous systems leading up to Homo sapiens, so that their opposites could
be extended or maximized by evolution.

Take just one property. As I write, I see a man out in the field opposite
who is patently drunk. He is standing in the wind and his shirt is flapping.
I see through binoculars that his shirt is sharply detailous (Ze., rich in
detail). I know perfectly well that, in his approximate stupor, this
sharpness that I see is wasted on this man. Yet it is my belief that such an
ability to remain aware of sharpness and detail is an essential to the
development of higher ideas in a human mind. That is, it seems to be a
trait of Man that he is able to sustain and control the processes of his mind.
