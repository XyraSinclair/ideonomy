THE DIRECTION OF NERVOUS EVOLUTION
Pat Gunkel

It is frequently remarked that evolution is not rectilinear, unilinear,
progressive, correlative, very explanatory, simple, direct, overt, equifinal,
or even very apparent caenozoologically or palaeozoologically. Naturally
this comment is often directed to remote taxa, eg bird versus mammal or
amphibians versus fish, or to close taxa, eg primate versus carnivore or
intraprimate, in which case the answer may be partly yes and partly caution
before all the evidence is in which may in fact show the above traits, such
as parallelism, convergence, homology, and seriation, by a new conceptualization
of structures and functions or merely by relatively good congruence or
clustering. In the case of close or even remote taxa the signifigance of
the remark may only be relative, an admonition in connexion with intensive
comparisons or as to small details in the absence of any referral to or
quantification of greater similarity. Moreover the things on my introductory
list are often confused by the receiver or the speaker and the speaker may
in any case only be referring to an interesting possibility or an unresolved
mystery that attachs some romance, A survey of all biota in all their bodily
aspects and lifeways, including the insecta, is apt to leave one very much
surprised at the degrees of similarity and nondissimilarity and at the additive
and conservative rather than transformative and innovative qualities of past
evolution; furthermore, one gains the impression that nature is rather
unimaginative and certainly unconscious, developing and adding traits almost
accidentally and apathetic to the progression and perfection of these traits
and their sum. Not only is one of the most encouraging things to one trying
to understand the basis of the mind the unchallengeable fact that nature
has developed such a mind, but also the apparent conclusion that what she
has managed she has managed despite herself, her slovenliness, randomness,
