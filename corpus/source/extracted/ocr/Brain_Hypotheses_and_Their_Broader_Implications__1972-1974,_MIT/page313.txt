THR PROBLEM OF BUILDING A NERVOUS SYSTEM
Pat Gunkel

So far as I can see the principal problem lies in building artificial neurons
with analogue and parallel memories with capacity in the neighborhood of man's.
Naturally it might be possible to improve certain things cae internal to
the neurons or quantitatively superior thereto in other ways ‘and to in this way
reduce the requirements internal to the neuron referred to above. There is good
reason for supposing that it has been design alterations that has given man his
quantitatively or qualitatively superior mind; in this case it happens there is

equally good reason for thinking that along these or in other dimensions the
artificial mind could be superiorly designed to man’s, even vastly so, and then
the requirements for various parts of the nervous system (such as neurons or
areas of the cortex) could drastically fall and greatly simplify tasks of mimiery
of parts and whole of man's intelligence (for, if, say, man's intelligence uses
design changes for catalytic jumps, then, reflexively, for the first tine what
was before an integral kind of intelligence could be fractioned into partially
intelligent entities, so defined). (Very good examples for the points about
redesign are found in the fact that the rate of thought and ontogeny should be
enormously accelerable in artificial minds. There is extremely good reason for
supposing that evolutionary changes only stepped over the portal in suddenly
giving man his kind of intellect and a vast universe of refinements and improvements
lay untouched beyond this portal. Specifically, the idea of "composite evolution”
or of man being the product of an acceleration or critical but arbitrary stage

in the evolution and adjustment of myriad modifications in aspects of nervous
functions and structures especially suggests the Likelihood of a universe beyond
the portal, gold and enormous.)

Onee such neurons are available I think it will be a relatively easy task to
modify them in order to produce the large, small, and many circuitries——essential
and inessential--that appear in man's brain against the background of the neurons
or that must appear against any such background to give intelligence transcending
anything that is done by computers today and equalling mane

But the question arises how difficult it will be, in practise, to produce a
technological componentry of the kind I have specified, particularly since there
is nothing analogous to be seen, even of the most germinal or suggestive kind,
and very peculiar factors condition the time when society will and can evolve it.

It is possible that small holograms could be produced, even with few problems.
Each microhologram or microgram would have to involve a certain complementary
machinery, the formal and practical complexity of which is indefinite. This
machinery would at least be concerned with the exchange of information between
cells or micrograms and perhaps with the energization or other maintenance of
the micrograms. If necessary, it might be well to include in this machinery
operations that according to some description diseretely or continuously and partly
or totaily erase or recast the aging memory in the micrograms to render them
palimpsests , abstractors, or compactors, perhaps performing these functions under
the control of afferent informations Over the long term, there is the question
of the lifetime of components under some sort of wear and whether this could be
adequate that no replacement would be necessary Or, if necessary, so that there
would be suitable replacement. It needs to not be forgotten that biological
neurons are living organisms and involve complicated machinery of repair,
energetics, and well-preserved maintenance codes starting with the gename.
