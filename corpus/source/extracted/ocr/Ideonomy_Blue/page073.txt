But this unlikely scholarly tradition at Airfix could not
continue. The model kit industry was under a heavy economic
cloud throughout the 1970s: Airfix ceased trading in January
1981, finally giving way under a ban by the European
Community on ‘‘warlike” illustrations on kit boxes. This
resulted in a compulsory redesign, costing £300 000 on Airfix
packaging. An Americati company, Palitoy, bought Airfix
and soon Humbrol British company, became the second
owner. Neit attempted to continue the proud Airfix
tradition of fine ship kits.

The last Airfix Classic Ship kit was a model of Captain
Bligh’s HMS Bounty, issued in 1979. We are unlikely to see
more. While some of the much-loved old kits of the 1960s and

1970s have been re-issued, the moulds are beginning to show
signs of age. and often require the modeller to hack away the
wall of “flash”, a thick plastic membrane resulting from
leakage in the mould. Some kits are now collectors’ items, sold
at collectors’ prices. Anyone with the kit of the liner, the
Southern Cross, should consider\keeping it in a bank.
