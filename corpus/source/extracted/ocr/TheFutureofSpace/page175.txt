The probability is over 30% that by 2020 payload will be deliverable into x
orbit from Earth's surface for no more than $10(1975) a pound. The possible
bases for this are discussed elsewhere and need not be repeated. But some
prominent ones can be referred to. By 2020 there may have arisen on Earth
the hydrogen economy foreseen by many men today. Hydrogen and air or oxygen
may be used throughout the economy of the world.for combustion and other purposes.
Such an economy might be a result of the emergence of fusion as an electric
and thermal resource throughout the world, and it will certainly reflect the
development and use of various fuel cells. A far greater number of products
and processes may use pure hydrogen then than now. In any case, the prevalence
of pure hydrogen may mean that its price will be minimal, so low, in fact,
that spaceflight will cost very much less than it does today. By 2020 fission
drives for spaceships of various sorts should have long been in use and have
become refined and economical. Controlled fusion drives may exist as well,
and, coming into use, drive down the cost of energy by simplifying ships and
operations and reducing the watt cost of power. Safety measures in spaceflight
may be relaxed, partly because of maximal safety, tremendous automation wil]
economize operations aboard ships and on the ground, fuel will be stored and
manufactured in Space, ships and facilities will be used at capacity, traffic
and freight will be so heavy as to have realized economies of scale, research
and development costs will have been dissociated from certain operations,
competition will have driven down and minimized prices, governments may be
producing minimal cost (zero profit or strategically overdeveloped) fuel
exclusively for Space, fuelless descent to Earth will be common, exomotive basers
will be in operation from Earth and Space, the cost of energy on Earth will

The probability strikes me as over 10% that by around 2020 such costs per
payload pound from Earth into orbit will have fallen to under $1(1975) as a
result, specifically (or perhaps specifically), of the development, perfection,
and use of a nuclear drive (say using a gaseous core reactor) in ascent from
Earth, and presumably return. A number of men have suggested this 2 orders
of magnitude reduction in payload pound costs from 1975, including men with
impressive credentials. But it has been contended that nuclear drives wil]
be too difficult or impossible for propulsion through Earth's pollutable
atmosphere and over Earth's populous ground and will therefore be limfted to
extraterran Space, or that they will be limited to extraplanetary Space as a
result of a low thrust impotent against gravity (in which case high thrust
nuclear engines would have to be developed, which seem perfectly possible).

The possibilities reviewed in the preceding paragraph might combine to lower
