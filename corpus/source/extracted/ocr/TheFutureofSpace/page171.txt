The Plenistic School of Physics which |! have said should arise beginning
around 2000, and eternally thereafter (according to one view), was, to be frank,
at least partly intended as something of a catchall or grab bag for the many
scientific and technological developments probable over such spans of time,
just as they have occurred in the past of physics. Of course it is at least
possible, it may even be probable, that marked acceleration may occur in the
rate of such developments, even an upward inflection in the curve of acceleration,
and occur, say, between now and 2020; physics, perhaps even technology in the
sense of applying what is discovered to practical affairs, may find itself
overwhelmed by plethoric discoveries of natural complexities, particles, effects,
laws, energies, forces, resources, &rc (&r this may occur at various later
dates). In any case, it seems reasonable that probably by around 2020 such
discoveries will have suggested means for extraordinary, or at least abnormal
or additional, communication (say over Space) via essentially new means: new frequencies
forces, energies, particles, effects, &r other relationships. Many possibilities
already exist in physics such that they can be named: black and white holes,
tachyons, neutrinos, muons, &c. It seems improbable that neither those things
nameable, nor those not and unknown, should by then be discovered and be of use
to communication, even if just by dint of clear siqn as to their eventual
possible or likely value, or achieved use on a laboratory scale or basis: physics
is far more progressive and a failure of such a development would at least be
inconsistent with the history of physics in 1975. There are many ways in which
one or more developments in the science and technology of communication might
have revolutionary significance for communication on Earth and in Space: the
natural flux of modulable entities through space might be found much greater
(more diverse, dense, powerful, useful, &rc) than previously assumed or known;
means for producing such greater fluxes might be found (in any of the ways
suggested); such fluxes (in any of the ways suggested) might be found (sooner
or later) to already carry a certain measure of extrasolian communications; &rc.
It might be found by then, in line with a Plenistic physics, that determinism
in nature is much greater than thought, that atoms are much more subdivided
than previously believed, that the functioning of nature is what might at first
seem extraphysically complex, that new mathematics allow far more powerful or
different communicable effects (with a hint that mathematics is unencumbered
by the supposedly ''physical'' limitations of the world), &r the like. Gerald
Feinberg, a prominent sponsor of hypothetical tachyons, deems them only 5% probable,
and is more skeptical still about their possible use by ourselves to transmit
or receive messages, particularly since such uses might seem irreconcilable
with the conventional idea of causality in physics, although this is an area of
heated controversy. My own guess is that other particles somehow exceeding the
speed of light may well exist, that solutions to the seemingly paradoxical
effects of tachyons or their hypothetical uses are possible that are neglected
by current--and therefore naive--discussion, that paradoxes of an analogous or
even homologous kind may exist elsewhere in physics &r in nature, that the nature
of time itself is far different and more complex than currently assumed, that
“superinfinite'' (or more than infinite) time may exist and have a bearing on these
matters, that tachyons themselves may be more likely than currently assumed,
and that things in nature faster than light may even be necessary. It has been
remarked that the cosmological implications of actual tachyons would be
extraordinary since they would seem, in their preference for infinite velocity,
fundamentally indifferent to the current 10!9LY event-horizon for the universe,

luxons (particles limited to 0 deviance from c, the speed of light) and tardyons
(particles limited to velocities less than c). Martin Harwit has argued
