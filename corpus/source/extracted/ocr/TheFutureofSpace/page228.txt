Commentary on the preceding pair of lists.

The writer is able to suggest the distinctions between the abilities of
the human brain and current machines that he does on the basis of his
authority as a neurologist and his intense interest in computer intelligence.
The distinctions are sound. But certain ambiguities in the comparisons are
evident. These were intentional. Partly they reflect the discrepancy
between the state of the art in computer software and hardware in 1975 and
developments 1-5 decades hence, when software and hardware together will
experience immense quantitative and qualitative progress and reverse
inferior relationships to men prevailing today and in the interim. He
has scant doubt that 5 decades hence, in 2025, the computer will have
transcended human intelligence in every meaningful respect and probably to
an astronomic degree; certainly this will be the situation sometime in the
2lst century.

Partly the ambiguities just referred to reflect ambiguities inherent
in the measurements intitulated. For example, ''memory'' is a thing embodying
countless, often orthogonal, dimensions and always almost completely obscure
in its measure, or in the measures of these myriad dimensions. Therefore
it is in a way preposterous to make Pretentious comparisons between the
computer and the brain. No one knows how many "bits!! figure in human thought
or exist in human memory, ostensibly relating brain and machine--no one
has a meritorious reason for identifying a bit sum of "engrams'' exact to
a range of less than 21 orders of magnitude (or 70 octaves), and the range
could conceivably be infinite instead of finite, or permeated with far
more important considerations.

| have referred to human intuition as ''Hilbertian'’ as a stenographic
scholium for the possibility or probability that behind and basic to human
thought, memory, and insight is something like Hilbert space, or an infinite
dimensionality practically irreducible to a "bits" or information-theoretic
quantification and possibly fundamentally dissimilar to current discrete
computers. This carries with it the possibility that the memories returned
to Earth by astronauts amount to infinite gestalten, however poor their
subsequent use.

The new view of nature or world view propounded in this book, referred
to as The Efflorescent World View, complements the above and suggests that
much of nature may only at present be revealed to man, and much of Space
only to human astronauts. This of course jis an hypothesis, about which
readers will disagree. There is at least the chance that strong, subtle,
little-understood reasons exist for perpetuating men in Space or using
men and computers in sensible combinations.

A particular value of men, at least in the future, is that they can
Survey and assess large and idiosyncratic environments of data in a firsthand
and immediate way. Simultaneous speculative induction and passive and active
experimental deduction is thus possible; the compression of time for
empiriological researches is far more than linear for this quick or instantaneous
feedback, and the power with which landscape and experience are analyzed
far exceeds what would be possible in later assessing records brought back to
Earth or more or less instantaneously transmitted in awkward cybernetic ways.
This second handicap results, and has resulted, in a certain conceptual
blindness toward what obtains at the loci explored. This criticism will have
far greater force if large, permanent, professional, and theoretical teams
are eventually established at Spacial loci in place of mere machines.

Be it concluded that the debate so far over whether Programs in Space should
or could be manned or unmanned has not been very sophisticated, careful,
or decisive, and is a poor guide for policy.
