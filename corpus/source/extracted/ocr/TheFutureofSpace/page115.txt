One problem is that Mercury's mean velocity is faster than Earth's by more

than Earth's is faster than Saturn's, requiring great energy for interplanetary
commuting. The approximately Terran temperatures at a meter's depth and the
ready insulation of the ground, not to mention the accomodative length of the
night, would seem to make the subterranean mining and occupation of Mercury

far easier than in the case of Venus, and perhaps not hard at all. Perhaps
development will actually occur sooner and more rapidly than with Venus. Mercury's
proximity to the Sun might make it an outpost and midpoint for study and use of
our star, the site of a manned, or extraordinarily complex and sophisticated
mechanical, heliological laboratory, or of a solar power generation station
retransmitting its energy to Earth or other points in the Solar System. The
photonic flux density with distance from the Sun presumably obeys a simple
inverse-square law regardless of frequency, but the same presumably does not

hold for fermionic species, velocities, densities, energies, &c, so that energic
effects and utilitarian advantages might be far greater than the discrepancy
between Mercury's and Earth's distance from the Sun might suggest. Mercurian
radioisotopic mineralogy, and its prior telemineralogical and epiplanetary
mapping, might be especially interesting, say as compared with the other planets.
The degree of its differential superiority to these planetaries, in gross and

net economic terms, is uncertain: it might be negative, unity, fractional,
manifold, or over an order of magnitude. The rapidity with which these
circumstances would lead to exploitation is uncertain. It is not impossible

that recognizable subterranean life does or could exist on Mercury, but it would
be astonishing given our limited assumptions about the nature of life. Discovery
of elementary or fossil life on Mars, Venus, Mercury, &r comets would imply

life on the others is far more likely. There is a chance that life is far more
pervasive, various, and tenacious than is customarily thought or is suggested

by the Earth; ''open'' rather than ''closed'' definitions of life may be more
accurate or sooner or later timely. Life as we know it may be a special case

of a much larger set of actualities or possibilities, that might be variously
finite or infinite. Solar energy used on or relayed from Mercury would be
