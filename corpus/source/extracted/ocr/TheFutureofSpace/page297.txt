The Efflorescent World View set forth in this book renders it obvious that
such inconceivably greater numbers of men would, far from experiencing monotony
in the likeness, normalcy, simplicity, and convergence of their lives, at once
exhibit and experience a variety, divergence, elegance, complexity, variation,
individuality, &¢ unknown to our own. Let there thus be removed from mind
the contrary expectation of a cheapened existence and an exhausted planet.

The Efflorescent World View postulates that nature, science, art, existence,
and the future are infinite, progressive, superinfinite, hierarchic, &c.

Elsewhere in this book ! show why the resources of minerals, energies,
future industries, space, &C are already known to be adequate to accomodate,
without sacrifises in the quality of life, all of the superpopulations discussed
above. Of course the rate of growth of population should not exceed, in any
detrimental way, the concomitant growth of enabling means and circumstances,
but the world's current imbalance--itself finite and questionable--is most
likely to be irrelevant to the sane, self-controlled, and omnipotent civilization
that will arise over the next 100-200 years as the changed face of the human
race,

Yet the greatest pronatalist argument may be, simply, that potential people
have a right to exist. Even when revaluated for interactions with transitional
and final demographic quality (or the actual and unknown tradeoff of quality
at the extreme of quantity), and any justified concessions to the historical
peoples such as ourselves whose decision and Preparatory labor is critical
to the appearance of the exploded populations of men, it is obvious that men
now alive have the simul taneous obligation and opportunity to originate
posterities far larger than exist today as an accident of history, but a fortunate
accident to the egoistic degree that we ourselves exist as members of the
present enlarged global population, and would not exist had antinatal policies
prevailed in the past. When | was in my teens | used to take exquisite pleasure
in moving some object about on the top of a table for the simple reason that
this had as its direct consequence that some hand in the future was assured
thereby of changing its place in a deterministic fashion. At the same time,
this was an intellectual pleasure with a moral note since what really fascinated
me was the vivid illustration in this way of the obligations men have to their
posterities through being the sole agents predetermining the critical conditions
to be enjoyed by those posterities on the basis of selection and care amid
Present acts. I saw beyond this, furthermore, that the consequences were not
to be unique but on the contrary would multiply so as to eventually cover all
things in the worlds of these pathetically dependent but reincarnating posterities,
particularly as the acts in the Present were extraordinary and sprang from
a specific awareness of the operative mechanisms and the laws of time. | then
concluded that the distinction made by current men, my fellows, between things
in esse vis-a-vis things in posse or in futura was everywhere misconceived as
absolute and critical, whereas in fact man's rights and obligations extend
naturally to encompass these intangible but equally dependent things, that
the authority for our actions must be conclusions formed from the scrutiny of
an imaginary scales with the present on the right counterbalanced by the future
on the left. Be it instinct, opportunity, or obligation motivating us, every
man to some degree feels an idealistic devotion to his family, community,
nation, &. It may in fact be the great challenge of the next 100-200 years
that man anticipate the future and reset his actions to accord with the obligations
of a scientific and profound conscience to things in posse and in futura.
