A single visit to or tour of Space in a lifetime could well be an almost
universal object, and a novel custom, of mankind. Tourists turning over in
Space would inevitably and progressively shed some percentage of temporary
and permanent residents whose connections to those back on Earth would induce
others to come and tour, visit, and remain in Space. Clearly, increasing
relative and absolute numbers of Terran tourists and emigrants would represent
a self-acceleratory process. The growth of Spacial touristry and population,
the development of multifarious and self-sufficient industry in Space, the
accumulative growth of instantaneous facilities and plant, the returns to
things in Space through economies of scale, the improvements resulting from
the turnover of priorities, the tendency to long-term investment and overall
planning marking Space from the beginning, the organic and slow perfection of
things in Space, the gradual evolution by Space of an idiosyncratic and unique
self-justifying atmosphere, the progressive excitement of affairs in Space
and the pervasive spirit of progress and triumph, the expanding environment
of Space, the flux of men and things to more distant points in the Solar System,
and a million other factors will unquestionably cause Space to be a self-acceleratory
process of the first order. Those who once visited Space would tend to return
as a consequence of realizing the convenience of doing so, reasons for doing so,
out of memory and habit, out of curiosity about progress, out of increasing
ease of doing so, out of enlarged and residual curiosity as to more distant
and neglected points in Space, as a reaffirmation of their uniquity, because
of job opportunities consequent upon their experience, and for other reasons
besides.

The inception and growth of diaglobal flight over the next 25-50 years--
supersonic, hypersonic, rocket, and suborbital continental, intercontinental,
and antipodal shipment, travel, and commut ing-~should underly, stimulate, and
be stimulated by orbital and transorbital flight into Space. The fact that
half the energy necessary to go almost anywhere within the Solar System is
that necessary to escape from the geogravisphere, and that return to Earth
presupposes far less energy from many key loci in the Solar System despite
their distance, has as an important consequence that no great economic
discontinuity seperates geocentric from transterran Space and no overwhelming
economic warrant exists for confining industry, population, and touristry to
periterran Space and a convenient geosatellosphere. Moreover, falling costs
and swelling means in the years ahead will ease Spacial enterprise by so many
orders of magnitude that the few factors advantage apparently possessed by
periterran and cislunar loci will lose all marginal significance and be
overleaped by Sivaistic enterprise catapulting beyond into translunar Space.

It is not obvious in advance what sublime aspect the terraqueous Earth
will reveal from Space as seen by encircling tourists lounging at windows,
floating in bubbles, or hovering in Spacesuits. It must be considered that
such tourists will bring to what they see a long conditioning and an itself-
miraculous expectation as to what will be beheld. The sight they therefore
see at their climatic mecca will be a fascinating synthesis of spectacular
natural beauty, exquisite symbolism, incandescent expectation, shared experience,
and profound philosophical reflection. Beheld will be the fragility, purity,
infantility, self-enclosure, natural beauty, and singularity of the Earth.

As tourists graduate into Space the lesson will be learned that the Earth

is tiny, transcendable, not unique, and yet rich in suggestions for the future.
Few will witness these sights without coming away with some permanent alteration
in their concept of the universe, of the world, of others, and of themselves.
Indeed, on this basis alone a wise man might decree expense of no concern in

the provision of this experience to the whole of mankind. No reason exceeds
that of the wisdom of a thing.
