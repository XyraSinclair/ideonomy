VARIOUS REMARKS ON THE HARDSHIPS ENCOUNTERED IN READING THIS BOOK

The present order and form of the book is not at all what it will be when
the book is done. What you have instead is something escaped from a
workshop, circulated at this moment for the value its content may already
have to some, criticism, and an idea as to the nature of the work.
Complaints are frequent about the vocabulary, grammar, punctuation, and
style. For example, the coined words are viewed as unnecessary and
pretentious. The writer is conscious of the harm they do by alienating

his readership and the difficulties they present to some fixed in their
mental habits. He is particularly aware of how ugly they must cause his
beloved manuscript to appear. He nevertheless persists in his ways,
retaining the old coinages and adding new ones, for reasons which he
considers, after all, primary. He specifically feels that when a field

is first developing and someone makes an effort for the first time to
summarize and project the future of that field, an opportunity and even

an obligation exists to define the vocabulary of that field, and, in the
absence of, or case of defect in, such vocabulary this even extends to

the proposal of a new vocabulary in service to the future needs of the

field and to present discourse. Of course a certain competence and care

is presupposed in the architect of the vocabulary. The new words must

have the several virtues of being precise, accurate, formed in accordance
with the rules and traditions of etymology, mutually illuminating,
necessary, sufficient to the needs, embodying an important concept, and
relatively graceful. But it must be admitted that all words have some
defects and limitations, absolute intolerance of which would preclude the
existence of language and the ready evolution of fields of thought.
Moreover, crudities present in the new vocabulary can be removed with

time. Above all, the words | coin in this manuscript are hardly intended

to obscure matters, to impress the reader in any illusory way, or as

cheap expedients behind which there is some ignorance of science or of

the English language. They are not employed decoratively. They are not
motivated by some frivolous whimsy. | have advanced the new words for
exactly opposite reasons: as scientific instruments, as statements of

fact, as bases for better discourse, as illuminating metaphors, and as
things of beauty for their suitability. He who devises the first words

must become an object of derision by those more keen to the frail novelty of
his inventions than sensitive to the needs of the world which the brave words are
designed to answer.

An objection to the grammar is that it fs too complex and laborious.

Some readers react as if they were undergoing torture. Particularly the
endless listing of possibilities, qualifications, and aspects of things
gives umbrage to the humble reader. Readers see such listing as stuffing,
repetitious or trivial, and certainly as awkward and difficult to digest.
Yet the writer takes special pride in his listing. He sees in it an honest
coverage of the dimensions, uncertainties, and presuppositions of things,

as exemplifying unprecedented analysis and respect for the real world.

He concedes the discomfort which may be involved in accepting the complexity
of the world, but he feels the discomfort must be forgotten and the
complexity faced.

Readers are appalled at the immense paragraphs and sentences. It is doubted
that the writer has ever heard of indentations and periods, or bothers to
read what he has written. The writer replies, airily, that classical writers
achieved nobility of style with no attention to such arbitrary rules as flourish
today and pulsate in the marketing of books to increasingly undisciplined
publics. He is content to let the size and shape of his sentence and paragraph
be governed by the natural modulations of thought and the felicities of the moment.
