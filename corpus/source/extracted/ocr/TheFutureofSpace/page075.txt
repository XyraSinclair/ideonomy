The first serious use in Space of various electrostatic and electromagnetic
drives will occur in 1995. Known possibilities for electrostatic propulsion
include a nuclear reactor, photovoltaic cells, or solar collectors as the
power source; the Brayton gas-turbine cycle, Rankine turbine cycle, semiconductors
(thermocouples), or thermionic diodes for converting the power; and various
ionic or colloid devices for the thrust. Known possibilities for electromagnetic
propulsion include the thermal adiabatic arcjet, magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) arcjet,
magnetoplasmadynamic (MPD) arcjet, or electropulsed methods. The characteristics
currently assumed for electrostatic (ion) propulsion are a aha impulse
of 5,000-60,000 second, a thrust-to-weight ratio of 10 °-10°7, and a typical
thrust duration of months. The characteristics assigned electromagnetic systems
are a specific impulse of 5,000-25,000 second, and ditto. Clearly these drives
would seem to have special ranges of applicability. Per contra, chemica 2
propulsion involves a range of specific impulse of 120-450 second, a 10 °-10
thrust-to-weight ratio, and typical thrust duration of minutes; fission propulsion,
respectively, 500-1,100 second, 102-!, and minutes. Given their uncertain
nature, feasibility, cost, developmental requirements, drive characteristics,
applications, trade-offs, &c, it is really a little early to comment on future
events related to such drives. Even so, they may be the key to important
developments, and they are widely discussed.

1996 may be taken to stand for the year when controlled fission drives will
become operational in spacecraft &r atmospheric assistance thereof. Possibilities
include gaseous core, solid core, nuclear-electric, huge particle-reflecting
hemispheres, &c. Such drives could come into Spacial and subspacial use far
sooner were there not the opposition existing. Nuclear drives will greatly
expand unmanned and manned interplanetary flight and operations. The imminence
of controlled fusion and fusion drives in 1996 will have a complex positive and
negative bearing on fission developments in terms of both power generation and
propulsion in Space.

Pulsed nuclear ('"Orion'') propulsion will be tried out in Space in 1996 with
fission bombs. The blast effects of small, repeated atomic explosions wil]
be used to propel space vehicles. High instantaneous accelerations, loss of
fissionable fuel, and radiobiological hazards are serious problems that have
to be solved. Nuclear pulses may ultimately be used to propel planetesimals
of mineralogical value elsewhere in the Solar System without radiation hazard.
