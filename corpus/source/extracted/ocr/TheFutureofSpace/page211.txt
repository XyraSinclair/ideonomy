ideas hopelessly far-out, and others find them so ordinary as to be dull.
Scientists see no value in fiction, per se. Previous revolutionary progress
in science and technology is evidently viewed as traditional, or else as
having by now exhausted nearly everything that is possible. The writer
replies that the ideas are intended as imperfect but analogous substitutes
for things which will actually occur but are not presently comprehensible.

He claims that his manuscript arises out of a clearer imagination of our
ignorance and limitation. He asserts that important germs of later
developments in science always long predate the developments themselves

but are the bases for those developments. Further, he claims, lineaments

of the future always exist in the present and are characterizable by

men of genius. Such anticipations can play a role in bettering the future. {
He finds that many other defenses exist for what he does. — ,,
Disagreements with statements in the manuscript are common, even cyclonic. “| x
The dates are largely viewed as overoptimistic, even ridiculously so.

The writer in many cases agrees since he intends to correct the manuscript

on the basis of later impressions and the internally significant framework
created by the book. He feels, however, that readers are fallible as

well: they extrapolate current gloom, they disregard scientific and
technological progress, they fail to take into consideration demographic

and economic progress, they omit the 3rd dimension any view of the future
must have by incorporating effects of rates of specifically exponential
progress in all these things, they do not consider the probable ultimately
autocatalytic nature of what man will do in space (and they forget the
multiplicative and nonlinear relationships between many of these things),
they unfortunately bare their ignorance and poverty of imagination, they
readily assume antagonistic relationships, they view without emotion what
will be fueled by emotion, they do not consider possible accelerants

of space enterprise nor do they consider the importance of human values,

they fail to read--much less analyze and consider--the pages which are
before them (whereas the nature of the book specifically requires continuous,
considerable, arid determined reading of entries, chapters, and the whole;
even rereading), they react to the ideas for their very newness and
Strangeness, they react to the ideas for their intrinsic and ineliminable
difficulties, they infurfate themselves as they proceed as if the writer
were some sort of ogre, they extrapolate (in the writer's opinion) a

naive view of nature, they fail to combine the mutually critical parts

of the book, they presuppose no remedies will be supplied at a later date

by the industrious author, they caricature him as a fool, they content
themselves with superficial appearances and with what they see as symptoms,
they do not repair nor consider the larger significance of what they later
discover to be their mistakes, &rc. They do much else besides. The

dates used in the early projection found in the early manuscript assume
